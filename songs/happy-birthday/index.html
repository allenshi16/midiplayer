About Happy Birthday to You

Happy Birthday to You is one of the most beloved public-domain melodies available to play online. Happy Birthday to You originated as a classroom greeting song called "Good Morning to All," written by sisters Mildred J. Hill (melody, 1893) and Patty S. Hill (lyrics) in Louisville, Kentucky. The "Happy Birthday" lyrics were adapted from the original by the early 1900s. The song was subject to copyright claims for decades — Warner/Chappell Music claimed copyright until 2015, when a federal judge ruled the copyright was invalid, placing the song firmly in the public domain.

As a public-domain composition, Happy Birthday to You can be freely performed, arranged, and studied by musicians of all levels. The melody has endured for generations because of its memorable contour and emotional resonance.

Musical structure and analysis

This arrangement is written in C major at 110 BPM. The melody is in 3/4 time with a distinctive pickup note (the "Hap-" of "Happy"). The form is a simple 16-bar song in C major. The melody uses a call-and-response pattern: "Happy birthday to you" (call) appears three times, with "Happy birthday, dear [name]" (response) providing contrast before the final resolution.

Difficulty level and practice tips

Beginner. The melody is within the C major scale with a comfortable range. The 3/4 waltz feel and pickup note are the main rhythmic challenges. The "dear [name]" section requires a small leap that beginners should practice slowly.

Start by clapping the rhythm without playing — the pickup note (anacrusis) is the trickiest part. Once the rhythm is comfortable, play the melody hands separately. The key change for "dear [name]" should be smooth — practice the transition. Try playing in different keys to accompany different voice ranges.

Did you know?

Before the 2015 court ruling, restaurants and businesses had to pay licensing fees to use Happy Birthday publicly. Warner/Chappell collected an estimated $2 million per year from the song. The ruling saved filmmakers, greeting card companies, and event organizers from royalty payments.

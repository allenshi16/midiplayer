About Beethoven's Fifth — Opening Motif

Beethoven's Fifth — Opening Motif is one of the most beloved public-domain melodies available to play online. The opening four notes of Beethoven's Symphony No. 5 in C minor, Op. 67 (1808) are arguably the most famous four notes in classical music. The short-short-short-long rhythm (da-da-da-DUM) has been described as "Fate knocking at the door" — though this attribution comes from Beethoven's secretary Anton Schindler, whose reliability is disputed. The motif pervades the entire first movement, appearing in every section and development. The symphony as a whole traces a journey from C minor (struggle) to C major (triumph).

As a public-domain composition, Beethoven's Fifth — Opening Motif can be freely performed, arranged, and studied by musicians of all levels. The melody has endured for generations because of its memorable contour and emotional resonance.

Musical structure and analysis

This arrangement is written in C minor at 108 BPM. The opening motif (G–G–G–E♭) is one of the most economical themes in music history — just four notes. The rhythm (short-short-short-long) is the defining element. The motif is immediately repeated a step lower, then developed through sequential patterns. The entire first movement is built from this tiny cell through relentless variation and development.

Difficulty level and practice tips

Beginner. The four-note motif is simple to play. The rhythm is the main challenge — the three short notes followed by the long note must be precise. The C minor key uses three flats (B♭, E♭, A♭), which may challenge very young players.

The rhythm is everything. Practice "short-short-short-LONG" as a rhythmic pattern before adding pitches. The three short notes should be exactly equal in duration, and the long note should be precisely three times as long. The motif should sound urgent and dramatic. Did you know?

During World War II, the BBC used the opening motif of Beethoven's Fifth as a symbol of victory — the short-short-short-long rhythm matched the Morse code for "V" (three dots and a dash). The motif was played before every BBC broadcast to occupied Europe.

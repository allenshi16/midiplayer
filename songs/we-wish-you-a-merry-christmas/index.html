About We Wish You a Merry Christmas

We Wish You a Merry Christmas is one of the most beloved public-domain melodies available to play online. We Wish You a Merry Christmas is a traditional English West Country carol dating to the 16th century. It originated from the British custom of wealthy households giving Christmas treats to carolers on Christmas Eve — the song's demands for "figgy pudding" and refusal to leave until receiving some reflect this tradition. The melody was first published in 1935 by Arthur Warrell, though the tune and words had been passed down orally for centuries in the western counties of England.

As a public-domain composition, We Wish You a Merry Christmas can be freely performed, arranged, and studied by musicians of all levels. The melody has endured for generations because of its memorable contour and emotional resonance.

Musical structure and analysis

This arrangement is written in G major at 120 BPM. The carol uses a call-and-response structure. The main theme ("We wish you a merry Christmas") serves as the call, and the figgy pudding demands ("Now bring us some figgy pudding") form the response. The melody is in 3/4 time with a bright, major-key character. The repetitive structure makes it easy to learn and sing along.

Difficulty level and practice tips

Beginner. The melody is entirely within the G major scale with simple rhythms. The repetitive structure means you learn a small pattern and repeat it. The main challenge is maintaining even rhythm at the faster tempo.

The song's charm is in its rhythmic energy. Practice the "figgy pudding" section separately — the repeated notes should be crisp. The transition between the wish and the demand should be smooth. Try playing at different tempos: slow for practice, fast for festive energy.

Did you know?

Figgy pudding (also called Christmas pudding) is a real British dessert — a dense, steamed pudding made with dried fruits, spices, and often soaked in brandy. The tradition of carolers demanding treats dates to the medieval period, when the poor would go door-to-door during the Christmas season.

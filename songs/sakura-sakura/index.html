About Sakura Sakura

Sakura Sakura is one of the most beloved public-domain melodies available to play online. Sakura Sakura (Cherry Blossoms, Cherry Blossoms) is a traditional Japanese folk song dating to the Edo period (1603–1868). Originally composed as a koto (Japanese harp) piece for music education, it became one of the most recognized Japanese melodies worldwide. The lyrics celebrate the beauty of cherry blossoms in spring — a symbol of the transient nature of life in Japanese culture. The melody uses the Hirajoshi scale, a traditional Japanese pentatonic scale that gives it a distinctive, exotic character.

As a public-domain composition, Sakura Sakura can be freely performed, arranged, and studied by musicians of all levels. The melody has endured for generations because of its memorable contour and emotional resonance.

Musical structure and analysis

This arrangement is written in A minor (Hirajoshi scale) at 90 BPM. The melody is in A minor using the Hirajoshi scale (A–B–C–E–F), which omits the 4th and 7th degrees of the natural minor scale. This creates the distinctive Japanese sound. The form is a simple three-phrase structure with a slow, stately character. The melody moves in a stepwise pattern within the pentatonic framework.

Difficulty level and practice tips

Beginner to intermediate. The pentatonic scale means fewer notes to navigate, but the intervals may feel unfamiliar to players accustomed to Western scales. The slow tempo and sustained notes require good tone control. The main challenge is capturing the Japanese aesthetic — spare, contemplative, and beautiful in its simplicity.

Practice the Hirajoshi scale (A–B–C–E–F) before tackling the melody. The intervals between C and E (a minor third) and between F and A (also a minor third) are the distinctive jumps. The melody should be played with a gentle, contemplative touch — imagine the quiet beauty of cherry blossoms falling. Use our MIDI player to slow the tempo and study the scale patterns.

Did you know?

Sakura Sakura was originally composed during the Edo period as a practice piece for students of the koto, the traditional Japanese 13-string harp. The melody's pentatonic structure is perfectly suited to the koto's tuning, and the piece remains a staple of koto education today.

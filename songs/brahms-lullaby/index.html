About Brahms' Lullaby

Brahms' Lullaby is one of the most beloved public-domain melodies available to play online. Brahms' Lullaby ("Wiegenlied," Op. 49 No. 4) was composed by Johannes Brahms in 1868 as a gift for his former sweetheart Bertha Faber, who had just given birth to her second son. The melody was reportedly based on a lullaby Bertha used to sing to Brahms years earlier. The original German text begins "Wiegenlied" (cradle song) and the gentle 3/4 meter mimics the rocking motion of a cradle. It has since become one of the most universally known lullabies in Western culture.

As a public-domain composition, Brahms' Lullaby can be freely performed, arranged, and studied by musicians of all levels. The melody has endured for generations because of its memorable contour and emotional resonance.

Musical structure and analysis

This arrangement is written in C major at 90 BPM. The lullaby is in simple ternary form (A–B–A) in 3/4 time. The A section features a gentle ascending melody (C–E–G–A–G–E–C) that rises and falls like a rocking motion. The B section moves to the dominant (G major) with a slightly more animated melody before settling back to the tonic. The 3/4 meter and narrow range create an inherently soothing effect.

Difficulty level and practice tips

Beginner. The melody moves in gentle steps and small leaps within a comfortable range. The 3/4 waltz rhythm is natural and easy to feel. The left hand can play simple root-position chords (C, F, G7) or just bass notes on beat one.

The key to this lullaby is a gentle, singing tone. Play each note with a soft touch — imagine you are rocking a baby to sleep. Practice the waltz rhythm (strong-weak-weak) with your left hand first. The melody should flow naturally without any harsh accents. Use our MIDI player to slow the tempo for practice.

Did you know?

Brahms subtly embedded a hidden message in the accompaniment: the viola part quotes a Viennese popular song that Bertha Faber used to sing to him. It was his musical inside joke — a personal reference woven into one of the most public and beloved melodies ever written.
